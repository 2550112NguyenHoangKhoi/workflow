It is often argued that universities should equip graduates with the practical skills demanded by employers, while others maintain that higher education exists to pursue knowledge for its own sake. Although both views have merit, I believe that a university should balance the two, with a slight emphasis on intellectual development.

On the one hand, there are compelling reasons for universities to focus on employability. Tuition fees have risen considerably in many countries, so students understandably expect their degree to lead to a well-paid job. Furthermore, economies depend on a steady supply of qualified engineers, doctors and accountants; if graduates lack relevant skills, employers must spend heavily on training. For example, many technology companies now complain that computer science graduates are unfamiliar with the tools used in industry.

On the other hand, reducing universities to vocational training centres would be a serious mistake. Knowledge pursued for its own sake has historically produced breakthroughs that no one could have predicted, such as the theoretical physics that later made modern electronics possible. Moreover, subjects like philosophy and history develop critical thinking and communication skills, which are valuable in almost any career and in civic life. A narrowly practical curriculum may also become outdated quickly as technology changes.

In conclusion, while universities have a responsibility to prepare students for the workplace, their deeper purpose is to cultivate curiosity and independent thought. In my opinion, the most effective institutions combine rigorous academic study with opportunities for practical experience, such as internships, so that graduates are both thoughtful citizens and capable employees.
